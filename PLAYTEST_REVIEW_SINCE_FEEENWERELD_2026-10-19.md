# Changes since Feeënwereld: experience review — 19 October 2026

The changes are broadly beneficial to the experience the group wants: distinctive places, approachable unusual NPCs, improvisation, lasting consequences and connected scenes. The strongest improvements preserve what happens to people and places, allow ordinary conversation, and give the adjudicator more room to complete an intent. However, the current build still sometimes turns an entertaining proposal into repeated qualifications or a lost action. The technical foundation has improved more consistently than the moment-to-moment experience.

**Scope and baseline.** Reviewed current source, the differences from `cb9dd11` and `c076c21`, relevant plans, saved Feeënwereld turns, later campaign samples and all published cyberworld turns 0–10. Feeënwereld happened between large commits and already had a map, structured factions and a staged adjudication/narration pipeline. Those systems should not all be counted as additions since the run. Its later turns also already had a separate movement component.

Used a read-only online backup at `/tmp/gather-feeenwereld-changes-review-2026-10-19.sqlite`. Current gameplay-source fingerprint: `07c2c637f95fb8c1`. Cyberworld (`f67b71e4-9d11-4886-81df-06a45335667b`) turns 2–10 match that fingerprint; its opening and turn 1 use predecessor `d1b2f3870351f9a6`. This provides direct evidence of current behavior beyond descriptions in implementation plans. Other inspected samples include Kraterwereld, Underwaterworld, Warhammer and spacepirates; their older builds help explain why follow-ups were needed.

Feeënwereld used `gpt-5.6-sol`, cyberworld uses `gpt-6-sol`, and their briefs, players' immediate interests and encounters differ. This is not a controlled comparison of enjoyment or model quality. Specific code failures and observed outcomes can be verified; the overall effect on enjoyment remains an interpretation grounded in the group's stated preference.

The distinction between ordinary conversation and uncertain persuasion is useful. Cyberworld's failed refund forgery has an actual failed CHA receipt; its public example also gives a grounded favorable circumstance. Failure there is a legitimate game result. It should not be grouped together with the movement lost through formatting or a sale that has no supported transaction path.

**Finding 1 — high priority: compact recovery can still discard progress for formatting.**

Cyberworld turn 7 uses the current build. Kit submits: “Oke just sell them for 1 gold each, and then go to find vale.” The initial proposal leaves the sale unfinished but establishes movement through the open passage to Vale. Its first validation feedback is a request for local world progression, not an assertion that the passage is impassable.

The recovery response includes Kit's partial main action, successful movement and two public developments. It uses the compact action form encouraged elsewhere in the request: component facts and action-level aggregates are omitted. The regular adjudication path expands those fields with `normalizeAdjudicationStructure`. The recovery path at `server/providers.ts:1951` normalizes references but passes the compact patch directly to `applyAdjudicationRepair`, whose parser requires the complete action fields at `server/adjudication-recovery.ts:224`.

The exact saved response is rejected for missing `status`, `basis`, `fact`, `reason`, `receiptRefs`, `eventIds` and component facts. The final turn retains Bandit-7's contribution but blocks Kit's whole action; Kit has to ask to find Vale again in turn 8. The repair instructions do request a full action shape, but the surrounding compact-output instructions point the model in the other direction. The server already knows how to derive these missing fields.

An isolated replay reproduced the parser rejection. Applying only the existing structure normalization to that saved patch allowed it to parse and pass `validateAdjudication`, preserving Kit's movement and both progress events. The replay used the saved pre-repair encounter-check snapshot, not encounter checks later generated for the published fallback. No new dice or model requests were involved. This identifies a concrete fix candidate; it was not applied to runtime code or campaign history during this review.

Turn 9 also logs the same missing-action-fields recovery rejection, following separate faction-output errors. This is a recurring integration gap, not evidence that all partial recovery is ineffective: other actors' progress did survive.

For the desired experience, this matters more than another prose-length instruction. An allowed journey becoming “cannot carry out the submitted action” makes the world feel resistant for reasons players cannot reason about.

**Finding 2 — medium priority: current NPC memory can replace an older commitment at its limit.**

`journalWithNPCInteractions` appends exchanges to the NPC's current journal entry. When the combined text exceeds 1,500 characters, `server/npcs.ts:359` replaces it with the newest event fact. Full exchanges remain in completed history, but the older promise is no longer guaranteed to be present in the compact NPC memory supplied for later turns. The NPC registry's `fact` also represents its latest update rather than a separate commitment history.

A direct isolated check supplied an existing 1,473-character entry containing an earlier promise and a new 389-character exchange. The result contained only the new 389-character exchange; the promise was absent. Existing continuity tests demonstrate preservation while content fits, but the full passing suite does not establish preservation through this overflow.

The model is told to consolidate important promises, and recent history can still carry them temporarily. Therefore this is a verified memory-retention limitation, not proof that every long conversation loses its relationships or that old history is deleted. It is particularly relevant to reproducing a travelling companion such as the violin across a longer run.

**Finding 3 — current live play still favors qualifications over decisive developments in some scenes.**

Bandit-7 repeatedly invites people to spin, explains possible outcomes, offers a free spin, demonstrates and offers to work for Pip. Pip responds coherently, but much of turns 2–10 revolves around specifying odds, distinguishing cabinets, and explaining why no stake, prize or job arrangement exists. The NPC has continuity and a recognizable occupation; the encounter produces less transformation than the violin sequence or Romano's chapter-zero registration.

Some limits are appropriate: a player's descriptive concept cannot mint equipment, grant new powers or make an NPC consent automatically. The opportunity is for the adjudicator to establish a concrete NPC response or small consequential scene within those limits. A clear refusal, a harmless demonstration with a social consequence, a rivalry or a grounded agreement can move a scene somewhere. Repeatedly reaffirming what has not happened does less for the stated goal.

Kit's potion sale is another concrete stall. Turn 5 explicitly authorizes selling both potions for whatever the buyer pays, while trying to obtain a premium. A natural 1 correctly defeats the premium request, but the sale itself never completes. Turn 7's initial model response still leaves an explicitly accepted standing price unexecuted. The available adjudication tools handle checks, resources, encounters, loot offers and challenge rewards; there is no corresponding sale transaction that removes the offered inventory and credits only the seller. This is an unresolved capability gap, not a proven regression introduced since Feeënwereld. It nevertheless undermines newly encouraged trade opportunities.

The transcript supports identifying stalls; it does not prove that a particular additional prompt sentence caused the NPCs' cautious behavior. This review does not assume that every proposal should succeed or that a stranger must accept a dangerous wager.

**What remains only partly addressed.**

- **Returning through familiar terrain.** Ordinary travel still crosses one connection per turn. Gathering compresses travel to a companion, and contextual shortcuts can reconnect places, but a whole group repeating the same distant destination can still experience Feeënwereld turns 13–15 again. Tiles representing meaningful scenes helps; normal travel has no general multi-hop compression.
- **Long fights and repeated lost actions.** Automatic handling of incapacitated characters reduces waiting for impossible submissions, and authoritative enemy state prevents false defeats. Neither establishes that the eight-round spider encounter would now be shorter or more varied. The current cyberworld sample contains no combat to verify that outcome.
- **Pacing's extra work.** A two-meaningful-turn threshold still requests a development when due. That is now nonblocking and first arrivals are exempt from mandatory thread advancement, but the repair can add latency and must preserve ordinary conversations. Turn 7 demonstrates a remaining failure at this boundary.
- **Loot and encounter frequency.** Bonus search loot and random encounters provide rewards and activity. They are not direct evidence of stronger NPC relationships or more distinctive places. The current safe cyberworld scenes do not test dangerous-location encounter pacing; the design still relies on appropriate danger classification and encounter content.
- **Characterful invention.** The new prompts support concrete worldbuilding and distinct voices, but persistence alone does not generate another Snaarloze Viool or Voetnoot 7½. Feeënwereld's compatible motifs and unusual player characters supplied much of its identity. Future briefs should retain that kind of material without prescribing all future encounters.

**Verification.** Current TypeScript checking passed. All **967 tests in 43 files** passed. In addition, the saved compact-repair rejection and normalization-only replay were checked locally, and the NPC-memory overflow was reproduced directly. No browser run or new connected-model generation was performed for this review. Existing plans report previous isolated live scenarios and browser checks; those historical results are not presented as newly rerun here.

Current-build cyberworld turns 2–10 have a median of **78.2 seconds** and mean of **80.0 seconds** of recorded stage time, excluding player submission waits. The sample includes corrections and partial retention. Differences in model, actions and setting prevent attributing a cross-campaign speedup to these changes alone.

**Recommended order.** Preserve the stronger opening, dialogue, shared-scene and current-state work. Address compact recovery first, then ensure important NPC commitments survive bounded memory, then make explicitly supported mundane interactions complete without repeated negotiation. Improve familiar travel flow where it still requires repeated destination submissions. Further evaluation should exercise a recurring companion, a return to a changed place, an unusual but feasible social proposal and a quiet conversation, with actual outcomes assessed alongside consistency and timing.
